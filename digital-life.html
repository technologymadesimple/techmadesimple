The Digital Life.

Technology connects us, informs us, and makes everyday life easier. But how does it shape our mental health, our attention, and the information we leave behind?

A closer look at our online world WELL-BEING · ATTENTION · PRIVACY

IN THIS EDITION / 03 STORIES

01 / WELL-BEING Mental health & social comparison 02 / ATTENTION Time, focus & constant notifications 03 / PRIVACY Your digital footprint
The big picture

Technology connects us. But what does it cost?

We wake up to notifications, communicate through screens, learn from online videos, and turn to social media for entertainment. Digital technology is woven into how we study, work, socialize, and understand the world.

These tools offer real benefits. Social platforms can help people maintain friendships, find supportive communities, and discover information they might not encounter otherwise. Digital tools can also make education and essential services more accessible.

Yet convenience comes with questions. What happens when online comparison affects how we see ourselves? When a stream of notifications interrupts every attempt to concentrate? When information shared in seconds remains accessible years later?

Understanding these problems does not mean rejecting technology. It means looking beyond what our devices can do and asking how they influence the people using them.

A note on the evidence

Research identifies important associations between some patterns of digital media use and well-being. Outcomes vary between people, and a relationship between two factors does not automatically prove that one causes the other.

The goal of this edition is to explore the risks without ignoring the benefits and to help readers make informed choices.

01.
Well-being / Social media

When everyone else's life looks better than yours

Social media lets us share our experiences. It also surrounds us with carefully selected snapshots of everyone else's. Over time, that difference can affect how we feel about ourselves.

The comparison trap

A social media feed rarely shows an entire life. It shows selected moments: celebrations, vacations, achievements, carefully posed photographs, and exciting announcements. Ordinary routines, disappointments, and private struggles are much less likely to appear in the same way.

When we compare our everyday experiences with someone else's highlight reel, it can become easy to feel that we are falling behind. Appearance-focused content may also encourage unrealistic expectations about bodies, popularity, relationships, or success. Even when we know a post has been edited or carefully staged, repeated exposure can influence what we consider normal.

What the research tells us The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on social media and youth mental health summarized research linking heavier social media use with mental health concerns among young people. The advisory also emphasizes that researchers still need to understand which experiences are most harmful and which protective factors help. Read the advisory.

From comparison to emotional exhaustion

The effects are not identical for everyone. Some people find friendship, identity, inspiration, and community online. Others may encounter bullying, exclusion, pressure to respond immediately, or content that makes them feel inadequate.

For some young people, certain patterns of social media use are associated with anxiety, depressive symptoms, sleep disruption, or concerns about body image. These relationships are complex: existing mental health challenges can also affect how someone uses social media, and a person's circumstances matter.

The issue is not simply the number of minutes spent online. What someone sees, how they interact with it, whether they feel supported, and what online activity replaces in their daily life can all matter.

A feed is a collection of selected moments, not a complete picture of another person's life. A reminder for the next scroll

Finding a healthier relationship with social media

  • Curate your feed. Mute or unfollow accounts that repeatedly leave you feeling inadequate, distressed, or pressured.
  • Notice how you feel. Pay attention to whether particular apps leave you feeling connected and informed or anxious and drained.
  • Make room for offline connection. Protect time for friends, hobbies, exercise, and activities that make you feel present in your own life.
  • Talk to someone you trust. If online experiences are affecting your mood or self-esteem, you do not have to handle that pressure alone.
02.
Attention / Everyday habits

Living in a world that never stops asking for your attention

A single message takes seconds to read. A quick check can become twenty minutes of scrolling. And returning to the task we meant to finish is not always as easy as it sounds.

The interruption economy

Phones and apps are designed to keep us informed and connected. Messages, reminders, breaking news, likes, and recommendations can all be useful. But when several apps compete for our attention at once, it becomes difficult to decide what deserves it most.

Notifications invite us to switch tasks. Short videos and continuously refreshing feeds make it easy to move from one piece of content to the next without choosing a stopping point. When there is always something new to see, even a few minutes of free time can become an opportunity to check a screen.

The result may not be a complete inability to concentrate. Instead, it can be a day fragmented into small interruptions: stopping homework to answer a message, checking a notification during a conversation, or opening an app automatically whenever a difficult task feels uncomfortable.

When screen habits compete with sleep

Digital activities can also push bedtime later. A video that ends in thirty seconds may lead to another, while group chats and alerts can keep people mentally engaged long after they intended to stop.

Insufficient sleep can make it harder to concentrate, learn, manage emotions, and maintain a consistent routine. The concern is therefore not just screen use itself, but whether it repeatedly displaces rest, schoolwork, movement, and face-to-face time.

What public health guidance emphasizes The U.S. Surgeon General's 2026 advisory on harmful screen use highlights concerns around excessive screen exposure, sleep, development, and health. Its recommendations include tracking screen habits, setting boundaries, and creating opportunities for offline activities. Explore the advisory and resources.

Reclaiming your time and focus

  • Turn off nonessential notifications. Keep important calls and messages available while silencing apps that do not need your immediate attention.
  • Create focused periods. Put your phone out of reach during homework, reading, or other tasks that require sustained concentration.
  • Choose a stopping point. Decide in advance how long you want to spend on a particular app, and use a timer or app limit if it helps.
  • Protect your sleep routine. Set aside time to wind down without scrolling and keep your device from interrupting rest.
  • Distinguish useful screen time from automatic scrolling. A video call with a friend, a coding tutorial, and an hour of unplanned scrolling serve different purposes.

The aim is not to eliminate every distraction or measure every minute. It is to make sure your attention is going where you want it to go rather than wherever the next alert directs it.

03.
Privacy / Digital citizenship

The internet has a long memory

A photo, comment, location tag, or personal detail may seem insignificant in the moment. But once information is shared, you may lose control over who sees it, saves it, or passes it on.

What is a digital footprint?

Your digital footprint is the trail of information associated with your online activity. Some of it is visible: posts, comments, photographs, public profiles, and videos. Other information may be collected in the background, such as browsing activity, approximate location, device information, and interactions with websites or apps.

Companies may use collected information to personalize content, measure engagement, target advertising, or improve services. The specific information collected and how it is used depend on the platform, its settings, and its privacy policies.

This can be convenient. Recommendations can become more relevant, and saved preferences can make services easier to use. However, extensive collection and sharing of personal information can create risks when data is misused, exposed in a breach, or shared beyond what someone expected.

Why oversharing can have lasting consequences

Posting something publicly does not mean it will remain within its original audience. Other people can screenshot, download, forward, or repost content. Deleting the original post may not remove every copy.

Personal details can also reveal more than intended when combined. A school name, a photograph, a regular schedule, and a location tag might each seem harmless individually, but together they can expose information about someone's routine or whereabouts.

A digital footprint may also influence how others perceive us. Public posts and comments can resurface later, sometimes in contexts very different from the ones in which they were created. This does not mean one mistake permanently defines a person; it means pausing before sharing is worthwhile.

Privacy, cyberbullying, and control

Digital spaces can also be used to harass, embarrass, impersonate, or threaten people. Cyberbullying may spread quickly because harmful content can be copied and shared with a large audience. Victims may find it difficult to escape when the harassment follows them from school or work into their homes.

If this happens, save relevant evidence when it is safe to do so, block or report the accounts involved, and seek help from a trusted adult, school staff member, or other appropriate support. Threats, stalking, and the nonconsensual sharing of intimate material should be taken seriously.

Before you share something, consider not only who can see it now, but who could see it later. A practical privacy principle

Protecting your digital footprint

  • Review privacy settings. Check who can see your posts, contact you, tag you, or find your profile.
  • Share less identifying information. Avoid posting passwords, private documents, live location details, or information that reveals your daily routine.
  • Think before posting. Consider whether you would be comfortable with the content being copied or seen by an unintended audience.
  • Check app permissions. Ask whether an app genuinely needs access to your location, microphone, camera, contacts, or photos.
  • Secure your accounts. Use unique passwords, enable multifactor authentication where available, and keep recovery information current.
  • Remember that private is not the same as confidential. A private group can still contain members who save or share what you post.
A moment to reflect

Use technology on your terms.

Digital well-being is not about being perfect, deleting every app, or avoiding the internet. It is about recognizing your habits and making intentional decisions about how technology fits into your life.

01 / Check in with yourself After using an app, ask whether you feel informed, connected, entertained, stressed, or drained.
02 / Make your devices work for you Adjust notifications, privacy settings, and screen limits to support your priorities.
03 / Leave room for offline life Make time for rest, hobbies, exercise, and conversations that do not require a screen.
04 / Keep learning Technology changes quickly. Revisit your habits and privacy settings as new tools and platforms emerge.
